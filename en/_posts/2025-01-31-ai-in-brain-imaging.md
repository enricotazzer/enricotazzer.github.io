## Introduction

As we saw in the [previous article](/en/posts/introduction-to-ai-in-medicine/), Artificial Intelligence (AI) tools are bringing a breath of fresh air to medicine, with a clear push towards what is called personalized medicine.

In this article we will look at the personalized management of brain tumors from a high level, and then analyze the tools and the state of the art in this area in detail in the next article.

## Reaching an onco-functional balance

AI-based models have the potential to make a positive impact at the different stages of the diagnostic and therapeutic process. They are meant to work alongside the classic histological examination, through what is called the radiomic approach: combining quantitative analysis of radiological images, such as CT and MRI, with AI-based models to uncover information that isn't visible to the naked eye.

This 'futuristic' approach will allow a complementary, repeatable and non-invasive characterization of the patient's disease or lesion, helping oncologists and neurosurgeons choose the best treatment for the patient. Combining the histological and radiomic approaches is changing the paradigm of brain tumor management. The aim is to reach the patient's so-called **onco-functional balance**: the balance between the best possible treatment of the tumor, to increase the patient's life expectancy, and the preservation of the brain functions essential for a dignified quality of life.

## AI in neuroradiology

Although the technology is full of potential, the reality is that these tools are still very far from being part of the everyday workflow of radiologists and neuroradiologists. The biggest hurdle in this field isn't developing intelligent models, but getting them into doctors' daily work on a global scale.

I find the situation paradoxical: even though clinical studies that use a radiomic approach are growing day by day and show clear evidence, the approach seems to be sidelined.

The areas with the strongest results are lesion detection, differential diagnosis, non-invasive molecular characterization, defining the lesion's margins, and assessing response to treatment and prognosis.

## Focus on gliomas

Glioma is the most common type of tumor of the central nervous system (CNS). Gliomas arise from glial cells, which together with neurons make up the nervous system and nourish and support the neurons.

Glioma is a clinically and biologically heterogeneous disease, with several recognized histotypes and molecular subtypes, and clinical courses that range from slow growth with a benign prognosis to very fast growth with a poor prognosis. Because of this, a timely and accurate diagnosis is essential.

#### What role does AI play here?

Historically, these tumors were classified solely on their histopathological features, but the most recent classifications are adding genetic and epigenetic information too. The most important in this category are molecular markers and DNA methylation profiles.

The genetic and epigenetic profile defines the tumor's **molecular signature**, a kind of barcode that uniquely identifies the tumor and whose identification is crucial for treatment decisions.

A growing body of evidence shows how much capability and potential AI has in oncological imaging, to the point of extracting the **imaging signature**: unique, complementary and additional information for characterizing the tumor, essential to precision diagnostics without being invasive for the patient.

## Conclusions

The conclusion of this piece is the following: AI-based tools are the bridge between precision diagnostics and precision therapy, leading to a possible future in which medicine is tailored to each of us.
