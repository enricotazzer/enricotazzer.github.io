Integrating AI into health care has the potential to transform the industry, but it also raises ethical, regulatory and safety concerns. Having seen many possible benefits in the previous articles, we will now focus on the problems and risks these tools can bring to the industry.

Adopting AI in healthcare comes with ethical and regulatory challenges that need particular attention. They range from protecting the privacy of patient data to addressing algorithmic biases that can perpetuate disparities in health outcomes.

## Will machines surpass human diagnosis?

Although AI has the potential to make analyses more accurate, it isn't always better than human diagnosis. Thanks to clinical experience and logical reasoning, a doctor can reach the right diagnosis even in cases where the AI has made a wrong prediction.

The real risk, in my view, is believing that an AI system can completely replace medical judgment. Reality shows that AI performance, although consistent, can be better or worse than human performance depending on the context. That's why, as I have stressed several times, AI should be seen as a tool that supports diagnosis, not as a substitute for the human mind.

## Getting into the workflow

One of the main obstacles to the "AI revolution" in medicine is integrating these support tools into doctors' daily workflow. This is particularly complex for several reasons, including how little training healthcare professionals receive in using such systems. In highly specialized areas, such as the diagnosis and treatment of tumors, two main problems have emerged:

1. Limitations of the AI tool itself, often due to a lack of validation on real data, which makes it less reliable in complex clinical scenarios.
2. Difficulty of interpretation for the doctor, who, without adequate training, may not be able to understand or correctly apply the results the AI provides.

As things stand, cases of AI integrated into the clinical workflow are still few. As a result, the data available today has to be read with caution, since solid empirical evidence confirming the effectiveness and feasibility of this integration is still missing.

## How do we handle privacy?

The rapid arrival of new technologies in healthcare has sparked skepticism because of the risks of sharing data. Public rules are therefore needed to govern how data flows and who can access it, so that patients understand how and why their data is used.

This raises questions about privacy and its violation. AI systems should be protected against breaches of private data, to prevent psychological and reputational harm to patients. In this respect, the many attacks on the healthcare sector (the most attacked sector after manufacturing and technology, and services) should make us think about how important this is.

## Biases

The machine and deep learning models used in healthcare can be prone to algorithmic bias, which can lead to predictions based on non-causal factors and prejudices such as gender and ethnicity. Because these algorithms are designed and written by humans, human prejudices and biases get "baked into" the algorithm too, and the AI models' results end up with the same disparities.

## What happens when it goes wrong?

AI systems can make mistakes in diagnosing and treating patients, causing potential harm. Holding these systems accountable can be complex, because questions of legal liability arise over errors and how blame is shared. The lack of explanations in deep learning models can hinder both legal accountability and scientific understanding, putting patients' trust in the system at risk.

## Data availability and accessibility

Training AI algorithms for healthcare requires a large volume of data from many sources. However, accessing health data can be complicated, because it is fragmented across different platforms and systems. Technological progress and more advanced algorithms can help mitigate the problem of limited datasets.

## Social issues

The mistaken idea that AI will replace jobs in healthcare breeds skepticism and aversion towards AI-based technologies. However, introducing AI doesn't necessarily mean jobs disappear; rather, they get reorganized. To overcome skepticism and build trust in AI, it is essential to improve understanding of what it can do and to encourage an informed public debate.

## Closing discussion

Overall, AI offers enormous potential and will keep playing a crucial role in future healthcare decisions. Used wisely, AI can reduce the pressure on healthcare staff and improve the quality of their work, cutting errors and increasing precision.

However, integrating AI into healthcare comes with difficulties. Gathering enough data to train accurate algorithms is an ongoing challenge. Specific national and global guidelines are needed for the safe adoption and evaluation of AI technologies.

A crucial point will be regulation and ethics. International organizations and governments need to sit down at the table and decide how to handle errors. I'll leave you with some ethical questions:

- If there is an error, who do we blame?
- If we decide the machine is to blame, what do we do?
- What if the doctor gets it wrong and blames the machine?
- Can a machine be at fault for an error? If so, do we blame the programmer?

This is bar talk, but sooner or later someone will have to write the answers to these questions into rules.
